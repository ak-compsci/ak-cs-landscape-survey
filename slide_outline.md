# Slide Deck Outline: The State of CS Education in Alaska
**Audience:** Legislators, grant funders, school board members, DEED leadership, education stakeholders
**Purpose:** Establish the baseline picture of CS education statewide, make the case for action, and present a concrete ask.
**Suggested length:** 20-22 slides, 20-30 minute presentation

---

## SECTION 1: SETTING THE STAGE (Slides 1–4)

---

### SLIDE 1 — Title
**Title:** The State of Computer Science Education in Alaska
**Subtitle:** What We Know, What We're Missing, and What We Need
**Visual:** Alaska map with district boundaries (screenshot from Combined View, full-state view)
**Bottom line:** DEED logo, date, "Alaska CS Landscape Initiative"

---

### SLIDE 2 — Why Computer Science Matters
**Title:** Every Student Needs CS Skills
**Visual:** Simple icon grid — laptop, robot arm, stethoscope, dollar sign, ballot
**Talking points:**
- Computing is embedded in every industry: healthcare, energy, fishing, government, transportation
- CS is not just for programmers — it's problem-solving, logic, and digital literacy
- By 2030, an estimated X% of Alaska jobs will require computing skills
- Students without CS exposure are less prepared for college, workforce, and civic participation
**Speaker note:** Ground this in Alaska-specific context — oil &amp; gas data analysis, fisheries monitoring, drone operations, healthcare tech in rural communities.

---

### SLIDE 3 — The Alaska CS State Plan
**Title:** Alaska Has a Vision — Now We Need Data
**Visual:** Cover page of the Alaska CS State Plan (or key goal statement highlighted)
**Talking points:**
- Alaska's CS State Plan sets a vision: every student, every district, K-12
- Before we can make progress, we need to know where we're starting from
- This report is the first systematic effort to map CS across all 54 school districts
- Data is the foundation for policy, funding, and accountability
**Speaker note:** Reference the specific goals or pillars in the state plan that this data directly supports.

---

### SLIDE 4 — Two Data Sources, One Picture
**Title:** How We Built This Picture
**Visual:** Two-column layout — left: "2024-25 Course Data (State Reported)", right: "2025-26 CS Landscape Survey (District Self-Report)" — with a Venn diagram overlap in the middle labeled "Combined View"
**Talking points:**
- **2024-25 course data:** Required district reporting to DEED — covers all 54 districts — school-level course names — historical snapshot
- **2025-26 survey:** Voluntary district survey — 31 of 54 responded — richer detail on enrollment, grade bands, barriers
- Neither source alone tells the full story; together they reveal a clearer picture
- Important: "CS course" is broadly defined in both sources — includes CTE, vocational computing, and dedicated CS classes
**Speaker note:** Be upfront about limitations. This is a starting point, not a census. Credibility comes from honesty about what the data can and cannot tell us.

## SECTION 2: WHAT THE DATA SHOWS (Slides 5–12)

---

### SLIDE 5 — The Combined Map
**Title:** Where CS Is (and Isn't) in Alaska
**Visual:** Full-screen screenshot of the Combined View map (dark green, blue, amber, red, gray districts)
**Talking points:**
- [Walk through the color coding] Dark green = both sources confirm CS; Blue = 2024-25 records show CS; Amber = survey finds CS not in state data; Red = no CS; Gray = no data
- [Pause and let the map speak] Notice the geographic distribution
- CS is present in most larger population centers (Anchorage, Fairbanks, Juneau, Kenai)
- Large areas of western and interior Alaska are gray or red
**Speaker note:** Give people time to look at the map before talking. Point to specific regions. If presenting in person, consider showing the live interactive map instead of a screenshot.

---

### SLIDE 6 — The 2024-25 State Data
**Title:** 2024-25 School Year: What Districts Reported to DEED
**Visual:** Screenshot of the "2024-25 Course Data" map view (colored = has CS courses, gray = no records)
**Talking points:**
- ~34 of 54 districts reported at least one CS or CS-adjacent course in 2024-25
- Course categories include: Business Education, IT Services, Intro to Computers, AP CS, Media &amp; Graphic Arts, CS Programming, CS Special Projects
- **Important caveat:** These are broad categories. "Business Education" may include only Office Practices. "Media &amp; Graphic Arts" may or may not include web development.
- This data gives us reach, not depth
**Speaker note:** Consider showing a specific district popup to illustrate what "school-level" detail looks like in the map.

---

### SLIDE 7 — The 2025-26 Survey
**Title:** 2025-26 Survey: What Districts Told Us
**Visual:** Screenshot of the "Survey Data" map view + pie chart (Yes / No / Not Yet Surveyed)
**Talking points:**
- 31 of 54 districts responded to the Alaska CS Landscape Survey as of this report
- Of those: 28 say CS is offered, 7 say no, 1 is unsure
- But 23 districts haven't responded yet — and we don't know what they'll say
- The survey captures richer information: grade bands, enrollment ranges, reported barriers
**Speaker note:** Emphasize that 28 out of 31 responding districts saying "Yes" sounds great — but we need to hear from the other 23.

---

### SLIDE 8 — Grade Bands: Where CS Is Taught
**Title:** CS Is Concentrated at the High School Level
**Visual:** Stacked bar chart showing enrollment in CS vs. no-CS by grade band (K-2, 3-5, 6-8, 9-12)
**Talking points:**
- 9-12: ~26,654 students in districts offering CS at this level vs. ~16,438 in districts without
- 6-8: ~17,192 with CS access vs. ~13,980 without — getting closer to parity
- 3-5: ~3,329 with CS vs. ~28,101 without — a dramatic gap
- K-2: ~2,770 with CS vs. ~25,945 without — almost no elementary CS
- A student who doesn't encounter CS until high school has missed years of foundational thinking skills
**Speaker note:** The younger grades are where the biggest gap is. Early CS exposure matters for engagement and pipeline — especially for girls and underrepresented groups.

---

### SLIDE 9 — Enrollment Estimate
**Title:** How Many Students Have CS Access?
**Visual:** Large numbers — estimated students with CS access at each grade band
**Talking points:**
- These are estimates based on enrollment data from surveyed districts only
- High school level: the majority of districts with CS appear to offer it at grades 9-12
- Elementary access is extremely limited statewide
- We do not have data on how many students within CS-offering districts actually take a CS course
**Speaker note:** The key message: access is not the same as participation. Even in districts that offer CS, students may not take it.

---

### SLIDE 10 — Top Barriers
**Title:** What's Standing in the Way?
**Visual:** Horizontal bar chart of top 5 barriers (from survey data)
**Talking points:**
1. No funding for CS teaching positions — 14 districts
2. Scheduling conflicts with other required courses — 14 districts
3. No funding for CS curriculum/resources — 12 districts
4. Lack of qualified/trained teachers — 9 districts
5. Geographic isolation or small school size — 8 districts
- Two of the top three are about money. This is a solvable problem with the right investment.
- Teacher shortage and scheduling are systemic challenges that require longer-term solutions
**Speaker note:** Ask the audience: "Would your district be able to offer CS tomorrow if you had a fully funded position and a curriculum?" For many, the answer is yes.

---

### SLIDE 11 — The Teacher Shortage Problem
**Title:** You Can't Teach What You Don't Have Teachers For
**Visual:** Illustration/icon — one teacher, many hats (science, math, social studies, tech)
**Talking points:**
- 9 districts cite "lack of qualified or trained teachers" as a top barrier
- In small Alaska schools, one teacher may cover multiple content areas already
- Adding CS requires either a new hire (expensive) or training an existing teacher (time-intensive)
- There is no dedicated CS teacher certification pathway in Alaska — teachers cobble credentials together
- Virtual coursework and outside-in programs can help, but have limits
**Speaker note:** This is where workforce pipeline and higher ed partnerships come in — DEED can't solve this alone.

---

### SLIDE 12 — The Rural Reality
**Title:** Geography Makes Everything Harder
**Visual:** Map showing rural/remote districts highlighted (gray districts tend to be rural) — or a simple Alaska Borough map
**Talking points:**
- 8 districts cite geographic isolation or small school size as a top barrier
- Many of Alaska's smallest districts serve Alaska Native communities with already under-resourced schools
- A district with 3 teachers total cannot add CS without removing something else
- Travel for professional development is prohibitively expensive
- Connectivity varies — virtual CS coursework is not equally accessible
**Speaker note:** Rural CS equity is both an education equity issue and an economic equity issue. These communities deserve the same opportunities as Anchorage.

---

## SECTION 3: THE GAPS IN OUR PICTURE (Slides 13–14)

---

### SLIDE 13 — What We Don't Know Yet
**Title:** This Is a Starting Point, Not a Census
**Visual:** Simple "known vs. unknown" two-column list — or iceberg graphic (what's above water = what we know; below = unknown)
**What we know:**
- Which districts claim to offer CS (broad definition)
- Grade bands and enrollment ranges for surveyed districts
- Top barriers reported by districts
**What we don't know:**
- Course quality, standards alignment, curriculum used
- Teacher qualifications for CS courses
- How many students actually enroll in CS (not just available)
- Demographic breakdown of CS students (gender, race, disability, income)
- What the 23 unsurveyed districts are doing
**Talking points:**
- Knowing what we don't know is just as important as what we do know
- These gaps point directly to what we need to build: better data systems, more survey responses, and richer reporting

---

### SLIDE 14 — Discrepancies Between Sources
**Title:** When the Data Doesn't Agree
**Visual:** Screenshot of Combined View map zoomed in on a region showing amber (survey only) or red (survey says No but 2024-25 had CS) districts
**Talking points:**
- Some districts appear in the 2024-25 data but have not taken the survey yet (blue districts)
- Some districts told the survey they offer CS, but it doesn't show in the 2024-25 course categories (amber)
- A few districts may have added courses in 2025-26 that weren't in the prior year data
- These discrepancies are worth investigating — they reveal where our data systems don't align
**Speaker note:** Discrepancies aren't failures — they're signals. They point us to districts where a follow-up conversation will sharpen the picture.

## SECTION 4: WHAT WE NEED (Slides 15–19)

---

### SLIDE 15 — Recommendations Overview
**Title:** What Needs to Happen
**Visual:** Three pillars or columns — "Fund It," "Build the Pipeline," "Measure It"
**Talking points:**
- We've organized our recommendations into three areas
- Funding: direct and structural
- Teacher pipeline: short-term training + long-term certification
- Data: improve what we collect and how
- Each pillar has specific, actionable asks

---

### SLIDE 16 — Fund It
**Title:** Funding: The Most Solvable Barrier
**Visual:** Stack of bills icon or grant award graphic — keep it simple
**Talking points:**
- Create a dedicated CS education grant program for districts (suggest: $X per district annually)
- Priority set-asides for rural, small, and high-need districts
- Fund virtual CS coursework infrastructure — curriculum licenses + connectivity
- Consider a CS teacher stipend or loan forgiveness program for rural placements
- CS education spending should be viewed as workforce investment, not just education spending
**Speaker note:** If there are specific grant opportunities (federal, philanthropic) that stakeholders could support or help Alaska access, name them here.

---

### SLIDE 17 — Build the Teacher Pipeline
**Title:** Teachers: The Long Game
**Visual:** Pipeline graphic — from "interested adult" to "certified CS teacher"
**Talking points:**
- Create a CS endorsement pathway in Alaska teacher certification
- Partner with UAA/UAF/UAS to integrate CS pedagogy into teacher preparation programs
- Fund professional development for existing teachers (summer institutes, online coursework)
- Explore "grow your own" models in rural communities — train paraprofessionals and community members
- Investigate virtual CS instruction as a bridge solution while teacher supply is built up
**Speaker note:** This is a 3-5 year investment, not a quick fix. Starting now means having teachers in place when demand increases.

---

### SLIDE 18 — Measure It
**Title:** Data: You Can't Improve What You Don't Measure
**Visual:** Dashboard screenshot or data table icon
**Talking points:**
- Refine DEED course category definitions to distinguish dedicated CS from broad computing/technology
- Add CS reporting fields to mandatory annual district reporting
- Set a goal: 100% survey response rate by end of 2025-26 school year
- Track progress annually: number of districts with CS, grade bands covered, enrollment figures
- Collect teacher qualification and demographic data on CS students
**Speaker note:** Better data enables better arguments for funding, better accountability for progress, and better targeting of resources.

## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Format recommendations:**
- Use Google Slides or PowerPoint (avoid Canva for legislative/official presentations)
- Consistent color palette: Alaska blue (#1e3a5f), green (#15803d), amber (#d97706), red (#dc2626), gray (#94a3b8)
- Keep text minimal on slides — let charts and maps do the work
- Every data slide should have a source citation in small text at the bottom

**Map screenshots to capture (for static presentations):**
1. Combined View — full Alaska, all districts
2. 2024-25 Course Data — full Alaska
3. Survey Data — full Alaska (with pie chart visible)
4. Combined View — zoomed into Southcentral (Anchorage area)
5. Combined View — zoomed into Western Alaska (to show the gray/unsurveyed areas)
6. A specific district popup (combined mode) — showing both sources side by side

**Speaker preparation:**
- Practice the "discrepancy" explanation clearly — audiences will ask why sources don't match
- Be ready to name the 23 unsurveyed districts if asked
- Have a "what is CS" definition ready — expect legislators to question whether "Intro to Computers" counts
- Know your specific ask amount if fundraising — "$X to reach 23 more districts" is more powerful than "more funding"
